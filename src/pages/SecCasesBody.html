10 Landmark SEC Cases: What Enron and Nvidia Reveal About Stock Returns and Recovery

What happened to $100 invested before the news broke? We track ten stocks from day one through one year and show how long Nvidia took to regain its previous high.

By Kamal Khondkar · November 21, 2026

At Luckin Coffee, the SEC asked whether customers had bought the coffee at all. At Nvidia, the question was why customers bought the chips. Both cases matter to anyone forecasting sales. Only one involved allegations of fabricated retail transactions.

Here are ten cases selected for the different lessons they offer investors in U.S. public markets, including foreign issuers whose securities traded here. The order follows the history of the cases, with related examples grouped together. There is no meaningful dollar ranking across invented sales, inflated earnings, accelerated orders and penalties.

1. Enron · Who actually bore the risk?

SEC action involving former CFO Andrew Fastow ↗

An outside investor supposedly had $30 million at risk in Raptor I, a vehicle Enron used to hedge investments. The SEC alleged a side agreement promised the investor its money back plus $11 million before hedging began. The apparent independence of the hedge depended on a party whose downside Enron had agreed to absorb. Former CFO Andrew Fastow settled the SEC’s civil charges in 2004 and pleaded guilty to related criminal charges.

Investor question. When a company transfers risk, identify who must pay if the asset loses value. Look for guarantees and side agreements in the related-party notes.

SEC case record — Enron

2. WorldCom · What did the company buy with its line costs?

SEC complaint against WorldCom ↗

WorldCom paid other carriers to use their networks. The SEC alleged it transferred certain line costs into capital accounts, removing expenses from the current income statement. The arithmetic reverses the result: $2.393 billion of reported earnings before tax and minority interests minus $3.055 billion of omitted expense equals a $662 million loss. Those numbers cover 2001 in the original complaint.

Investor question. Ask what identifiable asset will still be available next year. A paid invoice establishes that money changed hands, but its service period determines which year bears the cost.

SEC case record — WorldCom

3. Waste Management · How long can a garbage truck earn its keep?

SEC complaint against six former officers ↗

In a civil complaint against six former senior officers, the SEC described inflated profits that included avoided depreciation. Extending a truck’s assumed useful life or raising its estimated resale value reduces the expense recorded each year. A fleet can therefore look more profitable even when its routes, customers and cash receipts stay the same.

Investor question. Compare asset lives with the replacement cycle and proceeds from actual disposals. Ask what operating evidence supports a change in estimate.

SEC case record — Waste Management

4. Xerox · Which part of a lease earned revenue today?

SEC accounting enforcement release ↗

A copier lease can pay for equipment, financing and years of service. How the contract price is allocated determines how much revenue appears up front. The SEC said Xerox’s accounting actions accelerated equipment revenue and lifted pre-tax earnings by about $1.5 billion over 1997–2000. Xerox agreed to a $10 million penalty and to restate its results.

Investor question. Follow one contract from delivery through service. A change in the allocation can lift current earnings without adding a customer or changing the lease payments.

SEC case record — Xerox

5. HealthSouth · Why did earnings keep coming in on target?

SEC release on the judgment against HealthSouth ↗

The SEC alleged that HealthSouth inflated earnings to meet analyst expectations. The 2005 final judgment against the company included a $100 million civil penalty and an inspector general reporting to its audit committee. The remedies addressed who could challenge the numbers as well as what the company reported.

Investor question. Compare reported earnings with facility activity, reimbursement trends and collections. Consistently meeting estimates warrants a question, but it takes transaction evidence to establish fabrication.

SEC case record — HealthSouth

6. Satyam Computer Services · Can the bank independently verify the balance?

SEC release on Satyam’s accounting fraud settlement ↗

In its settled civil action, the SEC charged the India-based issuer with overstating revenue, income and cash balances over five years. Fictitious invoices and false bank records made different parts of the financial statements appear consistent. Adding the overstatements on each statement would count parts of the same scheme repeatedly.

Investor question. Trace a sale through collection to an independently obtained bank confirmation. An invoice and a bank statement supplied by the same manager provide weaker corroboration.

SEC case record — Satyam Computer Services

7. Luckin Coffee · Who was on the other side of the sale?

SEC accounting fraud settlement ↗

The SEC alleged that Luckin used related parties to fabricate retail sales from at least April 2019 through January 2020. Its complaint also described inflated expenses used to help conceal the invented activity. Luckin agreed to pay a $180 million penalty to settle the charges. One amount describes alleged sales, the other the enforcement settlement.

Investor question. For a retailer, compare reported sales with store traffic, tickets per store and cash settlement. Ask whether apparently separate buyers share the same source of funds.

SEC case record — Luckin Coffee

8. General Electric · Where did the cash in the power business come from?

SEC settlement over disclosure violations ↗

The SEC’s order examined what GE told investors about its power and insurance businesses. One issue was the power business’s reliance on selling receivables to its financial-services business to generate cash. For someone forecasting industrial cash flow, collecting a customer bill early through an affiliate has different implications from receiving another customer order. GE settled without admitting or denying the findings.

Investor question. Read segment cash flow alongside intercompany transactions. Check how much cash would have arrived during the period without pulling future collections forward.

SEC case record — General Electric

9. Under Armour · How much of next quarter shipped early?

SEC settlement over revenue disclosures ↗

Across six consecutive quarters, the SEC found, Under Armour accelerated orders that customers had requested for future periods. Earlier shipments helped it meet revenue estimates and made future growth less certain. The order concerned how the company explained growth and that uncertainty. Under Armour agreed to a $9 million penalty without admitting or denying the findings.

Investor question. When shipment growth beats expectations, ask how much came from new demand and how much from delivering the existing backlog sooner. The next quarter starts with a different order book.

SEC case record — Under Armour

10. Nvidia (NVDA) · Who was buying the gaming chips?

SEC settlement over cryptomining disclosures in May 2022 ↗

A GPU sold through a gaming channel can end up mining cryptocurrency. In two quarterly filings for fiscal 2018, the SEC found, Nvidia reported material gaming growth while omitting the significant contribution of cryptomining. Nvidia discussed crypto demand elsewhere, which made the omission from the gaming discussion consequential. In 2022 it accepted a cease-and-desist order and a $5.5 million penalty without admitting or denying the findings.

Investor question. An investor projecting gaming sales had to estimate whether demand would persist as crypto economics changed. The issue was the explanation of completed sales. This historical order supplies no finding that Nvidia fabricated GPU sales or misstated its current AI revenue.

SEC case record — Nvidia (NVDA)

Where to start in your next filing

Choose the revenue or expense line that most changed your estimate of the business. Write down what would have had to happen outside the accounting system for that number to be right. A real customer collected the goods. A carrier provided future network capacity. An independent investor could actually lose its collateral. Then look for the disclosure that supports that event.

The ten records above have different legal postures. A complaint sets out allegations. A settled administrative order records the Commission’s findings and agreed relief. A company judgment and an individual executive’s criminal plea apply to different parties. The linked records identify which is which.
