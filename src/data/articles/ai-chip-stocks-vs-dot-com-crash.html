Are AI chip stocks following the dot-com crash?

By Kamal Khondkar

On November 25, 2026, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index traded 44.5% below its 52-week intraday high. By the close it had recovered some ground. That distinction matters when the question is whether chips are following the path they took in 2000. An investor watching the first selloff did not know October 2002 would mark the bottom. What had that investor actually seen by this stage?

March 2000: three investors, one entry date

Imagine three investors putting $100 to work on March 10, 2000. One bought the chip index, one the Nasdaq-100, and one the S&P 500. They entered on the same afternoon. What differed was how much of their money depended on one industry. The long-run chart compresses the early years. Zooming in brings the cost of that choice into view.

By October 09, 2002, the chip investor’s $100 was worth $16.06. The Nasdaq-100 investor had $17.60, and the S&P 500 investor had $55.68. Those are the outcomes we can see in hindsight. To compare that experience with 2026, we have to rewind the clock.

What broke investors’ confidence in 2000 and 2026?

On December 9, 1999, VA Linux offered shares at $30. Its first public trade was $299. It closed at $239.25. The same IPO produced a 697.5% gain for an investor allocated shares at the offer and a 20.0% loss for someone buying the opening trade. Chip stocks would keep climbing until March. The debut captured how much optimism was already in the price.

The Fed had been raising rates since June 1999. The Nasdaq Composite and SOX reached their closing peaks on March 10, 2000. Ten days later MicroStrategy announced it would restate its accounts. The shares fell from $226.75 to $86.75 in one session. On March 21 the Fed raised its target another quarter point to 6%. A high-profile earnings reversal and more expensive money confronted investors as the rally was already coming apart.

Anthropic arrived at a different point in the cycle. Its November 19, 2026 IPO priced at $83 and opened at $111.50. The first close was about 17% above the offer, leaving opening-trade buyers underwater. By November 24 the shares had traded below $83. A deal that investors hoped would steady the AI trade instead exposed how differently the same debut had treated its buyers.

Nvidia supplied the sharper near-term shock. On November 20, Reuters reported that Nvidia fell about 14% amid news of an SEC accounting investigation, while SOX fell about 11%. An investigation leaves the accounting question open. On November 23, Nvidia jumped 31.5% after Jensen Huang’s reinstatement as CEO. The abrupt reversal shows how sensitive prices were to company news in both directions.

The timing limits the analogy. VA Linux debuted before the dot-com peak. Anthropic debuted months after the 2026 SOX peak. MicroStrategy announced an actual restatement, while Nvidia faced a reported investigation. Those events help explain sharp changes in confidence. The longer chip downturn in 2000–02 also had a business explanation, which shows up later in the industry’s sales and inventories.

Sources: VA Linux trading · GAO on MicroStrategy · Federal Reserve decisions · Anthropic IPO reversal · November 20 selloff · Nvidia rebound.

How much of the one-day bounce survived?

Chip stocks bounced 14% in one session. That sounds like a recovery until you look at the next two closes. By Wednesday, SOX had given back 69% of Monday’s point gain. It still closed 4.4% above Friday’s low. Could this rally give way to a bigger selloff? During the dot-com bust, SOX rallied nearly 80% from an intermediate low before falling again. This time, 8,408.59 is the closing level to watch. A close below it would erase the rebound and set a new closing low for this selloff.

Was Nvidia’s 31.5% rebound a dead-cat bounce?

After Jensen Huang’s return as CEO, Nvidia surged from $125.53 on Friday, November 20, to $165.05 on Monday. In the next two sessions it slipped to $149.30. That erased 39.9% of Monday’s dollar gain, leaving the shares 18.9% above Friday’s close. As of November 25 the rally had faded, but buyers at Friday’s close still held a substantial gain.

Intel gained 10.2% on January 3, 2001, when the Fed unexpectedly cut rates. It first closed below its January 2 starting level again on January 17, 2001. By October 08, 2002, it had fallen 61.4% from the rally-day close. Intel gives us a completed example of a relief rally that failed during the dot-com bust. Its catalyst was a rate cut. Nvidia’s was a leadership change.

For Nvidia, $125.53 is the reference point. A close below it would erase the entire one-day rebound. A close above $165.05 would put the shares back above the rally-day close. At the article cutoff neither outcome had occurred.

Stop both selloffs on the same trading day

The next chart starts SOX at 100 at each cycle’s closing peak. November 25 falls 110 trading sessions after the 2026 peak. The equivalent session in the earlier cycle was August 16, 2000. Cover the historical line to the right of the dotted marker and compare the two readings.

SOX had lost 19.5% in the earlier cycle and 40.0% in 2026. The 2026 decline was 20.6 percentage points deeper at that point. This is the closest comparison the charts give us. The market paths beyond that point are known for only one of the two investors.

Why a rally could feel like a recovery in 2001

SOX finished calendar 2000 at 43.3 on the peak-equals-100 scale. Over the following year the industry’s customers worked through excess inventories. Worldwide semiconductor sales fell from $204.4 billion in 2000 to $139 billion in 2001. Lower unit volumes and lower average selling prices both contributed. Intel reported a 21% revenue decline of its own. Investors trying to judge a rally had to decide whether orders and pricing were turning with the share prices.

Historical sources: industry sales and inventory · Intel 2001 annual report.

During that rally, SOX gained 79.9% from its low. An investor who bought at the March 2000 peak was still down 52.1% even at the rally’s high. Both experiences were real. The rebound rewarded the buyer at the low while leaving the earlier buyer with a substantial loss.

How much of the AI chip rally survived?

SOX covers the broader semiconductor industry. The PHLX US AI Semiconductor Index (ASOX) follows companies in the AI chip supply chain, from chip designers and foundries to equipment suppliers. Its history starts in September 2024, so it gives us a closer look at the current cycle.

At its June 30 closing high, ASOX had gained 68.7% for the year. By November 25 it was 34.4% below that peak, yet still up 10.6% from December 31.
